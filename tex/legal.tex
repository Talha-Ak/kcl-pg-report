\chapter{Legal, Social, Ethical, and Professional Issues}
\label{ch:legal}

The deployment of AI systems in high-stakes domains
like healthcare and clinical decision-making
carries significant professional and ethical responsibilities.
This chapter analyses these issues using the BCS
Code of Conduct~\cite{bcs-code-of-conduct} as guiding principles.

\section{Public Safety and Wellbeing}

The primary ethical obligation of any AI tool, regardless of its domain, is to `do no harm'.
In the context of healthcare, this means ensuring that AI systems
do not compromise patient safety or wellbeing.
A misdiagnosis or failure from an AI system
to identify a critical condition can have dire consequences.

It is therefore imperative that clinicians are not misled by a system's outputs.
A single output should not be trusted blindly;
rather, clinicians should augment their decision-making
with the system's predictions,
using them as one of many tools in their diagnostic arsenal.
This is particularly important in the context of chest X-rays,
where multiple conditions may be present simultaneously,
and some conditions may be harder to identify than others.
Providing a prediction set serves as an indication of the system's confidence,
where a larger set indicates lower confidence, and therefore requires higher scrutiny.

As highlighted repeatedly in this report,
simple accuracy or coverage metrics are insufficient
to guarantee patient safety.
This work focuses on moving beyond coverage, controlling for more
relevant risk metrics like the False Negative Rate (FNR).
By ensuring that the FNR is controlled at a clinically acceptable level,
the system can be trusted to not overlook critical conditions,
thereby safeguarding patient wellbeing.

For the Chest X-ray domain, CRC's `in-expectation' control of the FNR
was shown to be a significant improvement over standard ICP.
However, for a patient, it means that while the system is reliable on average,
the risk could be higher for any single prediction.

LTT's \(1 - \delta\) high-probability guarantee
provides a more robust assurance.
It ensures that the system's performance will not degrade beyond the specified level,
providing a stronger guarantee for clinical use.

\section{Algorithmic Bias}

The datasets used in this work, while large, are not globally representative.
Models trained on these datasets may exhibit biases
that could lead to unequal performance across different demographic groups.
An avenue for future work would be group-conditional risk control,
where the risk is controlled separately for different demographic groups.
This would allow one to guarantee, for example, that the FNR is controlled
at a some level (e.g., 10\%) for all demographic groups,
rather than just on average across the entire population.

\section{Data Privacy and Security}

CXR datasets could contain sensitive patient information, potentially breaching their privacy.
All datasets used in this work (CheXpert~\cite{irvin2019chexpert},
NIH ChestX-ray14~\cite{wang2017chestxray}, PadChest~\cite{Bustos2020padchest})
are publicly available, de-identified, and anonymized datasets.
Any real-world deployment of such a system would require strict adherence
to data protection regulations like GDPR.


\section{Professional Conduct and Integrity}

This project was made possible by leveraging a rich ecosystem of existing tools and datasets.
In accordance with the BCS principle of respecting the intellectual property rights of others,
all external resources have been explicitly cited.
No claim is made to originality in the datasets or the underlying models,
but rather the implementation, rigorous comparative evaluation,
and analysis of conformal methods in the specific context of multi-label chest X-ray classification.
