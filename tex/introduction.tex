\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Black-box machine learning models are increasingly being integrated
into high-impact real-world settings, with healthcare being a prominent example.
These technologies hold the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy,
improve workflow efficiency, and support clinical decision-making.
However, in such safety-critical environments,
it is essential not only to evaluate predictive performance
but also to quantify model uncertainty in a meaningful way.

Standard evaluation metrics often fall short
by primarily focusing on point predictions
and lacking the ability to express the model's confidence in its outputs.
This lack of calibrated uncertainty can be especially problematic in domains where
incorrect decisions carry severe consequences for patient health.
For instance, the interpretation of \glspl{cxr}
is inherently a multi-label classification problem,
as a single radiograph may exhibit multiple concurrent findings.
In this context, standard classification accuracy often fails to capture the clinical nuances;
failing to detect a critical condition like Pleural Effusion (fluid build-up around the lungs),
for example, can have far more severe consequences than
incorrectly identifying the presence of Pulmonary Nodules (small lumps in the lungs).
As a result, developing trustworthy AI tools for \gls{cxr} analysis
requires methods that go beyond simple accuracy
and allow for the control of specific, clinically relevant risks.

Furthermore, when a radiologist incorporates a machine learning model into their decision process,
they typically consider only the model's outputs.
Any misleading or overconfident predictions could directly influence clinical decisions.
Understanding not just what the model predicts, but also how confident it is, becomes crucial.
A system that transparently expresses uncertainty
can help a radiologist calibrate their trust in the model,
weigh its suggestions appropriately, and potentially catch errors.

Addressing the critical need for dependable confidence measures
falls under the domain of \gls{uq}.
Within this field, \gls{cp}~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic}
stands out as a particularly appealing framework.
It offers a mathematically rigorous yet highly flexible approach
that can work with virtually any underlying machine learning model.
Rather than committing to a single prediction,
\gls{cp} generates prediction sets (a range or set of possible outcomes)
accompanied by a guarantee that the true outcome will be contained
within the set with a user-specified probability.
It has been shown that presenting prediction sets with such calibrated coverage guarantees
can improve human classification performance~\cite{cresswell2024conformal, zhang2024evaluating},
offering a valuable safety margin.
This model-agnostic nature makes \gls{cp} especially useful for
enhancing the reliability of complex black-box systems,
without requiring changes to the underlying architecture.
However, this fundamental guarantee of \emph{marginal} coverage
does not distinguish between different types of errors,
which is a critical limitation in clinical applications.

While the general coverage guarantees provided by standard \gls{cp} are broadly useful,
critical applications like medical diagnosis
often demand more nuanced control over potential errors.
As highlighted in the \gls{cxr} example, certain types of mistakes
(e.g., failing to detect an existing condition)
are far more detrimental than others.
This motivates the move to frameworks like \gls{crc}~\cite{angelopoulos2023crc},
which generalises \gls{cp} to control the expected value of a
user-defined, bounded, monotone loss function.
This allows a practitioner to directly control a metric
like the \gls{fnr}, aligning the model's guarantee with domain-specific priorities.

However, the guarantees of CRC are limited by two key assumptions.
First, the risk is only controlled \emph{in expectation},
which may not be a strong enough guarantee for safety-critical decisions.
Second, and more critically, it requires the loss function to be monotone.
This restriction prevents CRC from controlling many important non-monotone metrics,
such as the \gls{fdr}.

To address these limitations, the \gls{ltt} framework was proposed~\cite{angelopoulos2022ltt}.
By reframing calibration as a multiple hypothesis testing problem,
LTT can provide a stronger high-probability guarantee on the chosen risk.
Crucially, it removes the monotonicity requirement,
empowering it to control any risk function,
thereby unlocking the ability to manage a wider class of clinically relevant errors.

The distinct theoretical properties and practical trade-offs between these frameworks
motivate a comparative investigation.
Recognizing the need for guarantees that are both
statistically valid and clinically meaningful,
this project undertakes a systematic evaluation of \glsxtrlong{cp},
\glsxtrlong{crc}, and \glsxtrlong{ltt}
within the context of multi-label chest X-ray classification.
By implementing and testing these frameworks on key clinical risks,
this work aims to provide a clear analysis of their practical strengths,
limitations, and suitability for deployment in high-stakes environments.

\section{Research Aims \& Objectives}
\label{sec:aims-objectives}

The primary aims of this report are to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Implement and conduct a comparative evaluation of \gls{crc} and \gls{ltt}.} \\
    This project's foundational aim is to implement and systematically compare
    three distinct frameworks for post-hoc calibration of various deep learning models
    on multi-label chest X-ray classification tasks.
    The frameworks evaluated are:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{\glsxtrfull{icp}}:
        As a baseline for controlling the marginal coverage rate.
        \item \emph{\glsxtrfull{crc}}:
        A generalization designed to control the expected value of any bounded, monotone risk function.
        \item \emph{\glsxtrfull{ltt}}:
        A more recent framework that reframes calibration as a multiple hypothesis testing problem,
        enabling high-probability control of a wider class of risk functions, including non-monotone ones.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Evaluate the control of clinically relevant monotone and non-monotone risks.} \\
    A key objective is to investigate how effectively these frameworks can be tailored
    to manage different types of diagnostic error, moving beyond simple coverage.
    This includes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Controlling False Negatives with \gls{crc} and \gls{ltt}}:
        Assessing the ability of both \gls{crc} and \gls{ltt} to control the \gls{fnr}.
        This is a critical monotone risk in diagnostics,
        as minimizing it corresponds directly to the clinical imperative of not missing existing pathologies.
        \item \emph{Controlling False Discoveries with \gls{ltt}}:
        Leveraging the unique capability of the \gls{ltt} framework to control the \gls{fdr}.
        As a non-monotone risk, \gls{fdr} cannot be controlled by standard \gls{crc},
        and this evaluation will serve as a key point of comparison,
        demonstrating a major practical advantage of the hypothesis testing approach.
    \end{itemize}


    \item \textbf{Assess the Practicality and Nuances of Applying User-Defined Risk Functions.} \\
    This aim explores the flexibility and robustness of the frameworks
    when applied to more complex, realistic scenarios.
    The objective is to evaluate how performance is affected by:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Custom-Weighted Risk Functions}:
        Implementing and evaluating a clinically-weighted \gls{fnr} within the \gls{crc} framework.
        This novel risk function assigns higher penalties for missing more critical pathologies
        (e.g., Pleural Effusion) than less severe ones,
        assessing the framework's adaptability to nuanced clinical priorities.
        \item \emph{Generalization Across Models and Datasets}:
        Evaluating the performance and validity of the guarantees provided by all frameworks
        across different model architectures (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet)
        and imaging datasets (e.g., CheXpert, NIH ChestX-ray14)
        to understand their robustness to architectural choices and distribution shifts.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report Structure}

The report is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces
the theoretical foundations of \glsxtrlong{cp}, \glsxtrlong{crc}, and \glsxtrlong{ltt}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} outlines the experimental design, including research questions,
datasets, models, and evaluation metrics.
The practical implementation of the experimental pipeline is detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents and analyses the empirical findings,
comparing the performance of the three frameworks. The broader ethical, social, and professional
implications of the research are discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:legal}.
Finally, Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarises the key findings and contributions of the report,
and suggests directions for future work.
